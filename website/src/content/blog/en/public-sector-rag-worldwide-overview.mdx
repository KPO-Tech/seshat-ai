**An agent has to answer a simple question: which procedure applies today to this case? The
answer exists. It is probably in a PDF, perhaps in two, most likely in a version that is no longer
in force. The problem is not that the administration lacks information. It is that it has too much
of it, spread across too many places, and nobody can say which one is authoritative.**

*First article of a series of five. This one draws the panorama and states the thesis; the four
that follow open the hood.*

## 1. A worldwide problem, not a model problem

Document dispersion is not a French peculiarity. In France, according to a 2017 survey by the
Cour des comptes (the national audit court), only 16% of the State's archives are kept at the
Archives nationales; more than 60% are kept in the departmental archives <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>. The Archives
nationales hold 395 linear kilometres, 135 terabytes and 14.9 million digitised documents available
online <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>. These are not saturation figures: they are distribution figures. The document that
answers the question exists, but you have to know in which silo, in which version and under which
authority to look for it.

<Diagram {...corpusDisperseEn} />

Elsewhere, the same structure appears in other forms. At KIRA, in Germany, analysing an IT project
contract means cross-checking pieces spread over several documents <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>. At Digdir, in Norway, the tool
meant to extract measures from strategic documents first runs into the heterogeneity of formats
<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>. In Brazil, the public guide to RAG places revoked norms among the first risks: a document still
in the base may have lost all legal value <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup>. Three countries, three trades, one finding: the
corpus is the problem, long before the model.

How much does this searching cost? Nobody measures it. JuriScope, an investigative project of the
French Ministry of Foreign Affairs, mentions 40 to 60% of the time of some projects spent on
document research, with no protocol that would turn it into a general figure <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup>. The Cour des
comptes, for its part, establishes that existing mechanisms allow neither a firm commitment on the
productivity gains of digital projects nor a reliable measurement of them <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup>. This is an
important precision: the cost of the problem is documented qualitatively, not quantitatively.
Anyone who puts forward a percentage of lost time must say where it comes from.

> **What this article does not claim**
>
> It does not promise a demonstrated average gain: none is published. It does not promise
> automatic compliance: a RAG does not check that a norm is applicable. It does not promise
> sovereignty through hosting alone: an "on-premise" system can be less traceable than a cloud
> under contract. It does not promise infallibility: the systems that work are bounded. It gives
> no unit cost: no public case publishes one.

**A note on method.** The corpus of this article holds **23 RAG systems or projects** spread over
twelve countries, plus three European institutions (four projects). A source enters only if it
explicitly names RAG, or unambiguously describes the "document retrieval → generation" cycle.
Borderline cases (the *Pair* chatbot in Singapore, *Jüri* in Estonia, *Hankintavälkky* in
Finland) are cited as counter-examples, never counted as RAG. All pages were consulted on 19
October 2026; the statuses are those of the sources, not a field update.



## 2. Four things not to confuse

The word "chatbot" now covers four different things, and the confusion is the first source of
error in public projects.

| Object | What it does | Example | What it does not do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Information chatbot** | answers from public content or a decision tree | Ask Laura, El Paso, retired <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup> | does not cite a business corpus, does not abstain |
| **General-purpose assistant** | writes, summarises, free conversation | Pair, Singapore <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup>; L'Assistant, France <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10">10</a></sup> | does not guarantee grounding in documents |
| **Semantic search** | finds passages close to a question | Jüri, Estonia, according to a secondary source <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-11">11</a></sup> | does not generate a written answer |
| **Document RAG** | searches passages, gives them to the model, cites the sources | Assist <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12">12</a></sup>, China Maritime <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13">13</a></sup>, KIRA <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> | does not make the model infallible |

The most economical demonstration comes from Singapore. GovTech runs two products that everyone
calls "AI chatbots": **AIBots** and **Pair**. AIBots is described as a retrieval-augmented
generation system, on internal documents that can go up to the *Restricted / Sensitive* level
<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-14">14</a></sup>. Pair is a generative assistant, with no document retrieval described <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup>. Both are useful; only
one is a RAG. The difference is not cosmetic: it determines what you can demand of the system on
citation, permissions and abstention.

In Finland, *Hankintavälkky* illustrates another boundary. The assistant of the public
procurement portal is indeed in service, with about 700 conversations a month at the end of 2024
and favourable feedback <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-15">15</a></sup>. But no source describes its architecture as a RAG. Calling it "RAG" would
be a convenience of language, not a fact. The same caution applies to *Jüri*: the figure of 83%
of routine questions handled comes from a secondary source <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-11">11</a></sup>.

This clarification is not an exercise in vocabulary. A chatbot that does not cite cannot be
audited. A general-purpose assistant that does not abstain cannot be deployed on sensitive
procedures. A RAG that does not filter rights is not a document tool: it is a leak waiting to
happen. The requirements are not the same, and neither is the budget.

## 3. Three models of organisation

The panorama is not read as a maturity ladder, from the most advanced country to the least
advanced. It is read according to three ways of organising the service. One country can practise
all three.

<ModelGrid lang="en" />

**The shared platform.** A central operator provides the tooling, agencies bring their documents.
AIBots, in Singapore, claims 40,000 users, 115 agencies, 12,000 bots and more than a million
messages over the period August 2024 – February 2025 <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-14">14</a></sup>. North Carolina offers an information widget to
State agencies, hosted and maintained by NCDIT <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-16">16</a></sup>. In France, Albert API exposes generation,
RAG, vectorisation, reranking and OCR, with more than 70 public projects and more than 100,000
requests per week. These figures are the operator's, not audited <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-17">17</a></sup>.

**The business product.** One team, one corpus, one trade. Assist, in the United Kingdom, helps
public communicators write and review, over five explicitly maintained reference documents <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12">12</a></sup>.
China Maritime answers regulatory questions for seafarers and companies, on a base of 5,488
question-answer pairs <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13">13</a></sup>. Seoul Data Hub queries municipal public data in natural language
<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-18">18</a></sup>. KIRA analyses contracts of large IT projects, locally, on behalf of the ASDN <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>.

**The guide and the capabilities.** A document, not a system. GOV.UK AI Insights publishes two
guides on RAG, one of them on agentic RAG <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-19">19</a>, <a href="#src-20">20</a></sup>. Brazil publishes an introductory guide to RAG in
the public sector, which deals with revoked norms <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup>. Rwanda designed a Policy Navigator that won an
award at the AI for Good 2026 summit, with no established entry into service <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-21">21</a></sup>.

Each model solves one problem and leaves another untouched. The platform solves availability, not
the quality of the corpora poured into it. The business product solves precision on a narrow
perimeter, not reuse by other services. The guide solves the spread of practice, not execution.
This is why a country can have, at the same time, a national platform, a business product that
works and a guide: these are not three stages, they are three answers to three different
constraints.

The trap, when writing a panorama, is to rank countries. France has a platform (Albert API) and
business experiments (Loiret, RAGaRenn) <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-17">17</a>, <a href="#src-22">22</a></sup>. The United Kingdom has a guide (GOV.UK) and a product
(Assist). Singapore has a platform (AIBots) and a general-purpose assistant (Pair), which is not a
RAG. A table by country would give a false impression of progress; a table by model gives a
decision grid.

## 4. What services in production show

Six cases let us see what a public service in production actually publishes, and what it does
not.

**AIBots (Singapore).** The system is in production at government scale, on the government
network, with classified documents. What it publishes: adoption volumes. What it does not publish:
any measure of quality. We do not know what rate of correct answers it reaches, nor how permissions
go down to the passages <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-14">14</a></sup>. This is the paradox of the most cited case in the corpus: its success is
organisational, not technically documented.

**Assist (United Kingdom).** This is the most complete case. The algorithmic transparency record
describes a RAG combined with the GOV.UK Search API, with citations, over five reference
documents <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12">12</a></sup>. The service claims about three hours saved per week and 98% declared usefulness among
8,500 civil servants in July 2026. The method is not published. Hosting relies on AWS Bedrock,
with an "escrow" account that prevents the provider from accessing the data <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12">12</a></sup>. It is better
documented than average, and it is still not an evaluation: no question set, no recall, no exact
citation rate.

**China Maritime.** The Ministry of Transport publishes a rare figure: more than 90% announced
accuracy, over 3,570 answers, since 25 June 2025 <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13">13</a></sup>. The protocol is not published, the sample is not
described, the abstention rate is absent. The system provides for double checking and a handover
to a human. It is a real business service, with a figure, but a figure that allows no comparison.

**Seoul Data Hub.** 67,300 consultations, 13,800 users, about 80% satisfaction, according to a
city press release of February 2025 <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-18">18</a></sup>. The survey method is not published. Satisfaction measures the
experience, not accuracy. A user can be satisfied with a wrong answer if it is fluent.

**Ask the EP Archives.** The European Parliament makes more than 100,000 historical documents,
from 1952 to 1994, searchable, with machine translation into 55 languages <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-23">23</a></sup>. No metric is
published. It is a remarkable case for its language coverage and the age of the corpus; it says
nothing about the quality of the answers.

**ECBot (ECB).** The public chatbot combines predefined answers and a RAG layer for open
questions; it displays three main sources, and the IBM Watson Discovery infrastructure is located
in Germany and Romania <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-24">24</a></sup>. It is an example of a "second layer" RAG: the RAG is not the product, it
catches the questions the decision tree does not cover.

To which a useful counter-example is added: the American GSA declares a *Leasing Desk Guide Bot*
deployed, a *Pricing Policy Chatbot* in pilot and *LaborMatch IQ* in pre-deployment (three
statuses for three cases, none of which publishes a measured benefit) <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-25">25</a></sup>. The analytical mirror of
the federal inventory fills in none of the eight governance questions it tracks <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-26">26</a></sup>.

The American inventories illustrate the gap at federal scale. The GAO measures spectacular adoption
(32 use cases recorded in 2023, 282 in 2024) and, in the same report, the obstacles: technical and
budget resources, updating usage rules, privacy <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-27">27</a></sup>. NARA documents a natural-language search in
production and an automatic labelling of about two million documents, with no RAG architecture
described and no impact metric <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-28">28</a></sup>. The Department of Labor declares two tools deployed without
publishing a single figure <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-29">29</a></sup>. **An inventory says that a system exists; it does not say that it works.**

The common thread of all these cases fits in one sentence: **usage is proven, quality is not.** We
know how many people use them, how many questions are asked, how many documents are indexed. We
almost never know whether the answer is right, complete and applicable.

## 5. What the pilots admit

Pilots are more instructive than successes, because they say where the project stops.

**Minnesota.** The nebulaONE platform was meant to let State agencies prototype chatbots and RAGs
on data of *Low* and *Moderate* level. The project cost the TMF fund $64,000 and was **closed** on
11 March 2025, with no result published <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-30">30</a></sup>. This is not a documented failure: it is a stop without a
review. The difference matters to anyone looking for lessons learned.

**KIRA (Germany).** The prototype was presented in March 2025, with an MVP planned for the end of
August and daily use envisaged in the fourth quarter of 2025. No confirmation followed <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>. The case
is nonetheless interesting: it is *on-premise*, on sensitive contracts, with citations. It shows
that the confidentiality constraint can be handled, not that it has been at scale.

**Rwanda.** RISA's Policy Navigator received an international distinction in 2026, for an
announced perimeter of 50 agents and 24 institutions. Entry into service is not established <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-21">21</a></sup>. An
award is not a deployment; it is a recognition of design.

**Kenya.** GovBot is a 2024–2026 pilot, with a detailed architecture: ChromaDB, LlamaIndex,
PydanticAI, citations and confidence scores <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-31">31</a></sup>. It is one of the few cases where the architecture is
public. No usage or quality metric is.

**JRC (European Commission).** Two demonstrators: crisis analysis, in December 2024, and nuclear
safeguards, in September 2026 <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-32">32</a>, <a href="#src-33">33</a></sup>. Both are aimed at analysts; the second provides for human review. These are
demonstrators, not services.

**Japan and India.** SEABIS, the assistant of Japan's Digital Agency, was shown in a demonstration
on 25 June 2025, with citations and page numbers, without deployment <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-34">34</a></sup>. In India, the NFRA challenge
is a call for proposals, not a system <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-35">35</a></sup>.

**Network Rail (United Kingdom).** The case is technically accomplished and not yet in service.
The lessons-learned chatbot rests on Azure AI Search and Azure OpenAI, with citations; the proof of
concept took four to five weeks, the chatbot could be queried after two weeks, and nine weeks were
spent consolidating security, infrastructure and formats <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-36">36</a></sup>. No gain, no adoption, no accuracy is
published. A figure does circulate: the 29,000 hours a year and the roughly three million pounds
attributed to Network Rail concern another initiative, on the data of the Wales & Western region,
and must not be attached to the chatbot <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-37">37</a></sup>. This is the kind of confusion that turns a proof of
concept into an industrial success in a presentation.

The Norwegian case deserves a place of its own. Digdir does not publish a success: it publishes
**five failures** observed on 58 strategic documents: wrong page references, a perimeter not
respected, answers produced even when the base does not contain the information <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>. It is the only
public self-evaluation of this kind in the corpus. For a project manager it is worth more than ten
deployment announcements.

Two lessons emerge. First, **the move to service is a phase of its own**: a prototype that works
does not become a service because someone decides it should. Second, **stops are documented, causes
are not**. MILEVA was stopped on 31 January 2026 without publishing code, budget or usage
statistics <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup>; Intelligence Entreprise, at the DGE, stopped in 2022 after trying a semantic
engine on more than a million documents <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup>. These two stops say the same thing: the need was real,
the industrialisation was not.

## 6. The four missing pieces of evidence

This is where the panorama stops being a catalogue. Of 23 systems or projects, four publish a
quality or benefit figure: China Maritime, China NDA (a figure from a supplier file), Seoul and
Assist <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13">13</a>, <a href="#src-38">38</a>, <a href="#src-18">18</a>, <a href="#src-12">12</a></sup>. None publishes a reproducible protocol. None is evaluated by a third party.

<ProofMatrix lang="en" />

A fifth figure exists, French, outside the matrix: L'Assistant, a general-purpose assistant, with
75% of civil servants finding it useful and gains of 12 to 16% observed in discussion groups
<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-10">10</a></sup>. It does not change the finding, it confirms it: perception is measured, not document
fidelity.

**First missing piece: recall.** No case publishes the rate of relevant passages found on a set of
real questions. Yet it is the measure that governs everything else: if the passage is not in the
list of candidates, no model, no reranker can bring it back <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-39">39</a></sup>. A system can look excellent and
silently miss half of the difficult questions.

**Second missing piece: permissions.** Only one case explicitly mentions classified documents:
AIBots, up to the *Restricted / Sensitive (Normal)* level <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-14">14</a></sup>. No source describes how rights are
inherited down to the passages, nor the filtering at search time. Yet OWASP puts precisely this
point at the centre of its sheet on RAG security <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-40">40</a></sup>. The gap between the importance of the subject
and its public documentation is a result in itself, not a research gap.

**Third missing piece: cost.** Only two elements: $64,000 for the Minnesota pilot <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-30">30</a></sup> and billing
per million tokens in North Carolina, with no rate published <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-16">16</a></sup>. No cost per indexed document,
per query or per active user. Any comparison between architectures would therefore be an estimate
disguised as a result.

**Fourth missing piece: evaluation.** Four cases out of 23 systems, no published question set, no
exact citation rate, no abstention rate. Seven cases document citations, which is better than
nothing, but a displayed citation is not a verified citation <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12">12</a>, <a href="#src-24">24</a>, <a href="#src-3">3</a>, <a href="#src-31">31</a>, <a href="#src-18">18</a>, <a href="#src-34">34</a>, <a href="#src-13">13</a></sup>.

A reading precaution is needed here: "not documented" does not mean "absent". The matrix records
what the sources say, not what the systems do. An empty dot means that no public source describes
the function, not that it does not exist. It is a difference that hurried readers often confuse,
and that leads some to say that public systems have no permissions because nobody described them.



The sentence to remember is not a condemnation: **the public sector knows how to count users; it
does not yet know how to prove that an answer is right.** It is a work in progress, not a verdict.

## 7. Why RAG remains the right answer

If the evidence is missing, should we conclude that the technology is not worth the investment?
No. RAG answers a real problem, and it does so better than the available alternatives for
procedures that change.

<Diagram {...principeRagEn} />

**It makes the answer verifiable.** A model alone produces a plausible text; a RAG produces a text
accompanied by the passages that grounded it. The civil servant can open the document, check the
page, contest. This is the difference between an answer and an assertion <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-19">19</a></sup>.

**It lets you add a document without retraining the model.** A procedure published today can be
indexed tomorrow. This is what makes RAG suited to a living corpus, unlike fine-tuning, which
changes the behaviour of the model without guaranteeing freshness <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-19">19</a></sup>.

**It must not be confused with two other things.** RAG is not fine-tuning: one brings documents,
the other changes behaviour. RAG is not agentic either: GOV.UK recommends classic RAG by default,
and presents the agentic approach as longer, more expensive and harder to debug <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-20">20</a></sup>. These are
architecture choices, not degrees of modernity.

**The cases that work are bounded.** Assist works on five maintained documents <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12">12</a></sup>; China
Maritime on a base of 5,488 question-answer pairs <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13">13</a></sup>; North Carolina on the public content of agency
websites <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-16">16</a></sup>. None claims to cover "all the knowledge of the administration". The narrow perimeter is
not a limit of RAG: it is its condition of success.



One question remains that the panorama does not settle: is RAG the right answer for *every* need?
No. For a question whose answer fits in a structured sheet, a classic search is enough. For a
synthesis over an unstable corpus, a poorly governed RAG will add confidence to errors. The
technology is suited when the answer must be grounded, cited and kept up to date, not when it
merely has to be fluent.

## 8. What this changes for a public project

The IGF–IGAS–IGA report makes thirteen proposals for deploying AI in administrations: mapping use
cases, integration into information systems, reversibility, training, governance <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-41">41</a></sup>. A well-designed
document architecture directly serves several of them. It does not replace them.

Five decisions weigh more than the choice of the model.

**1. A narrow, business-oriented perimeter.** One corpus, one trade, a team that maintains it. The
opposite, "all the knowledge of the local authority", produces an index with no authority and an
answer with no one responsible.

**2. A real question set, before the tool.** Fifty to two hundred questions asked by real
civil servants, about 20% of which the corpus cannot answer <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-42">42</a></sup>. Without unanswerable questions, you do not
measure abstention; you measure the politeness of the model.

**3. Recall before generation.** Measure whether the right passages are found, separately from the
quality of the writing. A system that answers well from the wrong passages is a confidence
generator, not a document tool <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-39">39</a></sup>.

**4. Human validation on sensitive uses.** The cases that mention it (China Maritime, JRC, Digdir)
present it as necessary, not as a leftover of the transition <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-13">13</a></sup>
<sup class="cite"><a href="#src-33">33</a>, <a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>. A RAG that produces a decision without review is no longer an assistant.

**5. Stop criteria written in advance.** MILEVA and Intelligence Entreprise stopped <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup>; Minnesota
closed its pilot without a review <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-30">30</a></sup>. A project must say, before it starts, what would make it stop:
insufficient recall, a cost per query out of budget, unfunded document maintenance.

> **The five questions to ask before signing**
>
> 1. Which exact corpus, maintained by whom, how often?
> 2. On which real questions will you be evaluated, and how many are unanswerable?
> 3. How are access rights carried by the passages and filtered at search time?
> 4. What exact citation rate and what abstention rate do you accept?
> 5. What is the cost per indexed document and per query, and who pays for the update?

The fifth question is the one forgotten fastest. An index is not a one-off investment: it is a
document subscription. Assist holds only because five documents are explicitly maintained <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-12">12</a></sup>;
AIBots holds only because agencies contribute and update their corpora <sup class="cite"><a href="#src-14">14</a></sup>. Cutting the maintenance
budget means scheduling an index that degrades without anyone noticing.

None of these questions is about the model. That is the point: the model is the most visible and
least decisive part of the project.

## 10. Conclusion

RAG really does change access to knowledge in administrations: it makes searchable what was
scattered, and it makes verifiable what was asserted. But the transformation is not where one
expects it. It is not in the model, whose versions keep succeeding one another. It is in the
documentation: an identified, versioned corpus, whose rights are known and whose quality is
measured.

That is the lesson of the 23 systems surveyed. The best documented are not the most spectacular;
they are those of which one can say, precisely, what they do, on what, for whom, and with what
limits. The others produce usage figures and leave the most important question unanswered: was the
answer right?

At Seshat, we apply this rule to our own work: distinguishing what is available, what is
experimental and what is not measured. A document system that cannot say where its answer comes
from is not progress; it is one more black box.
